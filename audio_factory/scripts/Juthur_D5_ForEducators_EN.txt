If you are an educator working with Arabic-speaking children — this is for you.
Whether you are in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, the United States, or anywhere Arabic is taught — the challenge is the same.
How do you teach a child to read a language whose letters change shape, whose vowels are invisible, and whose sounds include phonemes that do not exist in English?
The answer Juthur proposes is this: Don't start with the letter. Start with the body.
Here is a practical starting point.
Before any reading lesson — three minutes. Place your hand on your chest. Four counts in. Six counts out — with the sound of haa.
This is the Anfas Ritual. It regulates the nervous system. It moves the child from the noise of the screen to the presence of the word. Three minutes. Every session. Without exception.
Second: choose one letter. One. Not a page. Not a lesson. One letter.
Place it in the sand tray — raised, textured. Ask the child to trace it with their finger. Eyes open. Then eyes closed. Then eyes open again.
This sequence — tactile, then imagined, then visual — is the core of TRF. And it takes four minutes.
Third: the body moves. Draw the letter large on the floor. The child walks its shape. Their whole body becomes the letter.
When the body has been the letter — the eye recognises it differently. Not as a shape to be decoded — but as a memory to be recalled.
This is not a supplementary activity. This is the lesson. The worksheet comes after — if it comes at all.
One final principle for practitioners: Presence, not correction.
Your job in a Juthur session is not to fix mistakes. It is to witness, to facilitate, and to hold the space.
When the child is silent — do not fill the silence. Four to ten seconds of silence is thinking, not confusion. Wait. And watch.
